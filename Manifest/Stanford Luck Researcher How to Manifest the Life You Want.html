Big Picture

Dr. Tina Seelig, a Stanford neuroscientist and author, explains that luck is something you can influence. She separates fortune (what happens to you) from luck (what you control). This is for anyone who feels unlucky or stuck. You'll learn a three-part framework: build your sailboat, recruit your crew, and hoist the sail.

Section A: Luck vs. Fortune

Core idea

Luck is defined as success or failure "apparently" caused by chance. The word "apparently" matters because underneath there are usually things you did to attract good luck. Fortune is what happens to you. Luck is what you control.

Key points

  • Some things are out of your control: height, birthplace, parents, a pandemic, war, poverty, systemic racism.
  • Many people use "luck" to humbly say they had nothing to do with a success.
  • Between what happens and your response, you get to decide how to react (a Victor Frankl idea).
  • Every decision opens a door. We are "always one decision away from a completely different life."
  • Luck begins when you act in the face of uncertainty.
  • Seelig says life is not fair, but we still have choices.

Example / Analogy

Mel met her husband at a bar when he said "make it two" to her drink order. Seelig met her publisher by chatting with the man next to her on a plane, then keeping the relationship going for about two years until it led to her book.

Why it matters

If you think luck just happens to you, you stop acting. If you see your part in it, you gain real levers to pull.

Section B: Winds of Luck and the Four Ways to Respond

Core idea

Opportunities are everywhere, like wind. What matters is how you engage with them. Seelig uses a picture of a house, weather vane, hot air balloon, windmill and sailboat.

Key points

  • Staying inside the house: you shut the shutters and don't notice opportunities.
  • Weather vane: you see opportunities but do nothing, like someone who has been "thinking about" a job or a dating profile forever.
  • Hot air balloon: you go where the wind takes you. This is reasonable at times, such as when exploring your interests.
  • Windmill: you capture the opportunities in your local area, such as being the best you can in a job.
  • Sailboat: you pursue a specific goal and seek the winds that get you there.
  • Seelig works with The Last Mile at San Quentin, which teaches innovation, entrepreneurship and coding to incarcerated people. She says people can change their attitude and actions to unlock possibilities they didn't see.

Example / Analogy

"You don't get a job, you get the keys to the building." Even in a job you dislike, you can bring energy, excellence and attitude to capture the opportunity around you.

Why it matters

The sailboat is the most active stance, and the rest of the talk shows how to build it.

Section C: Build Your Sailboat (Internal Work)

Core idea

The sailboat is your "prepared mind" (from Louis Pasteur's quote, "fortune favors the prepared mind"). It is the internal work you do to be ready for luck.

Key points

  • Know your core values. They are like the keel that keeps the boat steady. Without them, others can manipulate you into unethical or illegal acts.
  • Values can change over time. Mel's was safety and security when money was tight.
  • Think about your values in advance. There's a slippery slope: once you make one unethical choice, the next is easier.
  • Build a personal board of advisers to ask when something "doesn't smell right."
  • Keep the ability to walk away. Heavy debt can trap you in a job where you're asked to do something unethical.
  • Know the story you tell about who you are. It shifts depending on who you're with and how you feel.
  • Do small experiments every day and take risks outside your comfort zone.
  • Know your goals.

Example / Analogy

Seelig's first job was at a medtech company. Her boss sent her to a competitor's conference as "Dr. Seelig from Stanford." She never questioned it. The competitors grew suspicious, confronted her in an elevator and escorted her out. She says she hadn't thought about her values.

Why it matters

Clear values protect you from bad decisions and point you toward what you actually want.

Section D: Know Your Risk Profile (The Riskometer)

Core idea

Risk is more nuanced than "I am or I'm not a risk taker." Seelig's spider chart has six types of risk, each rated 0 to 10.

Key points

  • Physical: such as cycling fast or extreme sports.
  • Emotional: telling someone how you feel.
  • Social: giving a toast, a talk, or speaking in a meeting.
  • Financial: money risks.
  • Intellectual: trying something new, solving a hard problem.
  • You can stretch. A social risk that is a 2 today can be a 4, then a 6, with repetition.
  • Prepared people feel less risk. Venture capitalists try to squeeze risk out, like a skydiver packing a parachute.

Example / Analogy

Mel rated herself a 10 on speeches but a 2 on a red carpet, because she'd never done it. Seelig's $5 project: student teams got $5 and two hours to make money. Some ran a stand pumping bike tires (about $200), some sold restaurant reservations, and one team sold its three-minute class presentation slot to a company for $650.

Why it matters

The chart shows where your comfort zone ends. Stepping past it creates luck.

Section E: Recruit Your Crew

Core idea

"Luck seldom sails solo." Luck usually comes from engaging with other people. Ask for what you want, help others, and show appreciation.

Key points

  • Make asks small and easy to help with.
  • Send thank-you notes. Many people don't.
  • Generosity is a key trait of lucky people. Few people help others, so it stands out.
  • Helping may feel small to you but be very meaningful to the other person.
  • A warm introduction is one of the biggest gifts you can give.
  • Authentic compliments to strangers change the energy and open doors.

Example / Analogy

Oliver asked Seelig for five minutes about becoming a "luck coach." He sent a thank-you note and a list of ways he could help her, and she hired him as a research assistant. Nir Ayal sold solar panels and recommended a heat pump installer. The installer found out and began recommending him, and his business boomed. Mel, new in town with no friends, complimented the coffee shop worker, learned the name, and went back daily until she built a community.

Why it matters

Your network is where most lucky breaks come from, and generosity compounds.

Section F: Hoist the Sail (Take Action)

Core idea

"The harder I work, the luckier I get" is missing something: you need to know what kind of hard work. Seelig points to stirring the pot, stretching beyond your comfort zone, and being curious.

Key points

  • Luck favors those who ask questions (chapter 14 of her book). Curiosity leads to learning and opportunities you never imagined.
  • "Before something's your passion, it's something you know nothing about." Try many things and keep what works.
  • There are about 20 daily levers, such as asking, helping, showing appreciation, taking risks, resilience and resolving conflicts.
  • Luck is a long game, like investing: small deposits that compound.
  • Have the mindset that there's a prize in every room.
  • If you feel unlucky, do one thing this week to "stir the soup": apply for the job, or introduce yourself to someone in line.

Example / Analogy

Seelig compares luck to investing money. Each action is a small deposit that grows over time.

Why it matters

Choices today shape the choices you'll have later. Sitting on the couch won't meet the love of your life.

Key Terms Glossary

Luck (Seelig's definition)
Success or failure apparently caused by chance.
Fortune
The things that happen to you.
Prepared mind
Your internal readiness for opportunities (the sailboat).
Core values
The keel that keeps you steady and ethical.
Riskometer (spider chart)
A six-point chart for rating your comfort with different risks.
Personal board of advisers
People you go to when something doesn't feel right.
Stirring the pot (or soup)
Adding something new to your life to open doors.

Step-by-Step Process

Seelig's three-part method:

  1. Build your sailboat: learn your values, your story, your risk profile and your goals.
  2. Recruit your crew: ask for what you want, help others, show appreciation.
  3. Hoist the sail: take risks, stay curious, ask questions, and keep making small deposits over time.

Common Mistakes

Warnings

  • Confusing fortune with luck and thinking everything just happens to you.
  • Being the weather vane: noticing opportunities but doing nothing.
  • Not thinking about your values before you're pressured.
  • Talking yourself out of reaching out because you don't want to be a burden.
  • Skipping thank-you notes.
  • Walking past opportunities, like gold coins on the ground.
  • Taking on so much debt that you can't walk away.

Memory Aids

Five takeaways

  1. Fortune happens to you. Luck is what you control.
  2. Opportunities are like wind: you need a sail.
  3. Build the boat, recruit the crew, hoist the sail.
  4. Generosity and small asks bring luck back to you.
  5. Luck is a long game of small deposits.

Mnemonic

Action Items

Try these

  • Rate yourself 0 to 10 on each type of risk and pick one to stretch.
  • Write down your core values.
  • Name a few people for your personal board of advisers.
  • Send a thank-you note to someone who helped you.
  • Make a warm introduction between two people.
  • Give an authentic compliment to a stranger.
  • Ask for something small, like five minutes of someone's time.
  • Do one new thing this week to stir the soup.

30-Second Recap

Seelig says luck is what you control, while fortune is what happens to you. Opportunities are like wind, and you need a sail. Build your sailboat by knowing your values, story, risks and goals. Recruit your crew by asking, helping and thanking. Hoist the sail by taking risks and staying curious. Luck is a long game of small deposits, so start by stirring the soup this week.
